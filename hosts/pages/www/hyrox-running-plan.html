A Technique-First Running Plan · for HYROX

Run Stronger

Faster km times come from running that feels easier, not from gritting harder. This plan layers gently on top of your training — building comfort, economy, and confidence, one focus at a time.

“Think Easy, Light, Smooth, and Fast — get the first three, and the last one comes for free.”
How this works

Three simple rules

Run by feel, not by the clock. Hold one form focus at a time until it's automatic. And let speed emerge — form first, pace later.

New to the words here — cadence, RPE, run/walk, cadence pickups? The Appendix at the end explains each one in plain language. Worth a read before Week 1.

Effort, by feel (RPE)

Easy 3–4 — full conversation, nose breathing. Most runs live here.
Steady 5 — comfortable, working a little.
Comfortably hard 6–7 — short phrases only. The km repeats.
Hard 8+ — save it for race day.

The four levers (in order)

  1. Cadence & relaxation — quick comfort & pace gains.
  2. Aerobic durability — run all 8 km without walking.
  3. Brick running — run well on tired legs (the race itself).
  4. Goal-pace repeats — sharpen under the real structure.

Keep these close

  • Calves & Achilles are your canary. Mild tightness is normal adaptation. Sharp pain, or anything that changes how you run, means extra rest — don't advance.
  • Too much? Cut running intensity first — never the rest day.
  • Repeat any week that doesn't feel easy yet. This is a rhythm, not a deadline.
Phase 1

Comfort & Form

~Weeks 1–4 · build the habit

Make running feel good and install the technique while it's easy. Goal: build toward a comfortable, continuous 20 minutes — by run/walk, not all at once.

How to read every week below: the minutes are total session time, done as run / walk intervals (e.g. run 3 min, walk 1 min, repeat). Appendix

The words & the why

Plain-language explanations of the terms and ideas in this plan — none of them assume you've read a running book.

Easy · Light · Smooth · Fast — the four cues, in order

Those one-word cues in the corner of each early week (EASY, LIGHT, SMOOTH) come from a single idea in Born to Run: good running is built in four layers, one at a time, in order. You give all your attention to the current layer until it runs itself, then add the next.

“Think Easy, Light, Smooth, and Fast. You start with easy… Then work on light. Make it effortless… When you've practiced that so long that you forget you're practicing, you work on making it smoooooth. You won't have to worry about the last one — you get those three, and you'll be fast.”— Caballo Blanco, in Born to Run

Easy — Week 1, the foundation

This one is about effort, not form. Run slow enough to talk in full sentences and breathe through your nose (RPE 3–4). If that means very slow, or run/walk — perfect. Easy is the pace at which your body can actually absorb the training and your form has a chance to be good. Nearly everyone's instinct is to run too hard; “easy” is the discipline of going slower than feels natural. Apply it: pick a pace where talking is comfortable and hold it; the moment your breath turns ragged, walk.

Light — Week 2, minimal effort, minimal impact

Once easy is a habit, make the running feel light — like you're barely touching down. The lever is cadence: quick, short, soft steps so you skim the ground instead of pounding it. “Light” is the feeling; quick feet are how you get there. Picture the ground being hot, and spend as little time on it as you can. Apply it: cadence ~170–180, steps landing soft and quiet under you, hands and shoulders loose.

Smooth — Weeks 3–4, everything flowing forward

When light is automatic, smooth it out: no bobbing up and down, no jerky bits, all your energy going one direction — forward. Smooth comes from relaxation and from the pieces working together — quiet feet (Week 3) and relaxed arms (Week 4). Apply it: scan your body for anything gripping (jaw, shoulders, hands, calves) and let it go; aim for a ride steady enough that a glass of water balanced on your head wouldn't spill.

Fast — the payoff you never chase

You never train “fast” directly. Caballo's whole point: get easy, light, and smooth, and fast shows up on its own — because efficient running is fast running. When speed becomes the goal (Phase 3), it comes from doing the first three at a slightly higher gear, not from straining harder.

The four weekly form focuses — how to actually do them

Each early week also wears a form word in its corner — the one piece of technique to play with that week. Only ever think about one at a time: trying to fix everything at once fixes nothing. Here's how to do each, including on a treadmill.

Posture — “the column” (Week 1)

The tall, stacked, slightly-forward body line that everything else hangs on. Set it up: stand tall and imagine a string lifting the crown of your head; gently draw the lower belly in to level the pelvis (stop it tipping forward — this takes the over-arch out of your low back); line up ankle, hip, shoulder, ear; let the shoulders drop; keep the knees soft, not locked. The feel: long and stacked like a column — not slumped, not arched back. The lean: the slight forward lean comes from the ankles, tipping the whole straight column as one piece — never from bending at the waist. Common mistake: folding forward at the hips, or letting the hips sag back so you're “sitting” in your stride. On the treadmill: set the posture before the belt moves, then re-check it every minute — crown up, belly gently engaged, shoulders down.

Cadence — “quick feet” (Week 2)

Quick, short, light steps — the single highest-value habit in running. (The full reasoning is in “Cadence — why…” just below.) Do it: set a metronome or playlist to 170–180 and take a step on each beat; keep the steps short, landing under your hips. Common mistake: speeding the feet up but still reaching the foot out in front — the step has to stay short, landing beneath you, or you lose the whole benefit.

Soft landing — “quiet feet” (Week 3)

Land on the middle of the foot, softly, directly under your body, with relaxed lower legs. Do it: let the foot fall and “kiss” the ground rather than stamping; keep ankles and calves loose; think of lifting the foot back up off the ground rather than shoving off hard; land midfoot/flat, not way out on the heel. The test is noise: if you can hear yourself slapping or thudding, you're landing too hard or too far in front — aim to be nearly silent, like running on sand. Why it matters for you specifically: soft, quiet landings are exactly what protect the calves and Achilles, which is what lets you run more without breaking down. On the treadmill: you can hear yourself easily over the belt — use that sound as live feedback and quiet it down.

Arm swing — “relaxed arms” (Week 4)

Arms that swing easily and quietly set the rhythm for the whole body. Do it: bend the elbows about 90° and keep them there; swing from the shoulder, straight back and forth — not across your body; hands loose (imagine holding a potato chip you don't want to crack); shoulders down and relaxed. The link: arms drive legs — a quicker, more compact arm swing makes quicker feet, so if you want to lift your cadence, drive the elbows back a touch faster and the legs follow. Common mistake: arms crossing the midline (which makes you wobble side to side), hunched tight shoulders, or clenched fists. On the treadmill: if there's a mirror, check that your elbows travel straight back rather than flaring out, and that your shoulders look low and loose.

Cadence — why “quick and light” beats “long and strong”

Cadence is just how many steps you take per minute (counting both feet). It's the most important single thing in this plan, so it's worth understanding why.

When most people try to run faster, they reach the foot further out in front — a long, lunging stride. The problem: a foot that lands ahead of your body acts like a small brake on every step. It comes down hard on the heel, stops your momentum, and sends a jolt up through the heel, knee, and hip. It's slower and harder on the body.

Taking quicker, shorter steps — aiming for about 170–180 steps per minute — fixes this almost by itself. Faster turnover means each step is naturally shorter, so the foot lands underneath you instead of out front. That one change gives you: no braking, a softer landing on the middle of the foot instead of a hard heel-strike, much less impact on joints and tendons, and less wasted up-and-down bounce. The result is running that's gentler on the body and faster for the same effort — which is the entire idea behind this plan.

Here's the part people get backwards: your cadence barely changes. Once it's quick (~170–180), it stays about the same whether you're cruising or pressing — you don't speed up by churning the legs faster. In Chi Running's terms, your lean is the “gas pedal”: to go faster you let your whole body fall forward a little more from the ankles (the same lean as your posture work), and gravity pulls you along at a faster rate. Your stride lengthens on its own — think of it as shifting up a gear. Lean a hair more for speed, lean less to ease off; the feet keep ticking at the same rate, and speed is not a matter of working harder. (On a treadmill the dial sets your speed, so this matters most outdoors — but the feel is the same: lean a little more, don't work the legs harder.)

How to set the cadence itself: put a metronome app (or a music playlist) at 170–180 beats per minute and match your footfalls to the beat. It feels silly-fast for a few minutes, then clicks.

Run / walk

Run for a set time, walk for a set time, repeat for the length of the session. It's a proven method, not a beginner's crutch — plenty of experienced runners use it. The walk breaks let you bank more good-form running with less fatigue and far less injury risk, and they're the natural bridge from short runs to long ones.

The rule: grow the running portion only when the current one feels genuinely easy — and add an extra walk break any time, in any week. There's no week where a walk break is “cheating.”

Cadence pickups — and why they don't make you faster

A cadence pickup trains one specific thing — your legs' ability to turn over quickly — for one purpose: to make your base cadence of 170–180 feel easy and automatic. It is a practice drill, not a way to run faster. In fact, on a treadmill it changes your turnover without changing your speed at all — and that's exactly the point.

What it trains

New legs aren't used to ticking over at 170–180, so the moment you stop concentrating they drift slower — into a long, lazy plod. A pickup deliberately spins the legs a few beats above your cruising cadence for a few seconds, then you settle back. Visiting “quicker” makes 170–180 feel relaxed and normal instead of fast — you're widening your range so your comfortable home base creeps up toward target. It also drills pure quickness, the opposite of over-striding: you physically can't reach the foot out in front at 185–190, so the body is forced to keep the steps short and landing under you.

How to do it (treadmill)

  • At your easy belt speed, bump turnover to roughly 5–10 beats above cruising (e.g. ~175 → ~185–190) for 20–30 sec — tiny, quick, light steps, staying relaxed.
  • Then ~2 min back at your normal easy cadence to settle. Repeat for the count called for (“pickups ×5” = five).
  • A metronome nudged up a few beats — or just counting “quick-quick-quick” — makes it easy to find.

Why it does not make you go faster — the important part

On a treadmill the belt fixes your speed. Speed = cadence × stride length, so when you raise cadence your stride shortens by exactly the same amount and you stay put on the belt — same speed; you've just traded longer steps for quicker ones. You aren't running faster, you're rearranging the same speed into more, smaller steps.

And that mirrors the rule for running everywhere, not just on the belt: your speed over the ground is always governed by your lean, never your cadence. Lean a little more and gravity pulls you faster (your stride lengthens); lean less and you slow — while cadence holds steady around 170–180 the whole time. A pickup is the one moment you deliberately push cadence off that steady number, purely as practice — and it's useful precisely because it proves the two are separate levers: you can crank your turnover right up and go not one inch faster.

The mental model: pickups make quick legs feel normal; the lean makes you fast. Two different jobs — never confuse them. And they're quick and light, never hard: effort and breathing barely change, so pickups cost almost no fatigue.

RPE & the talk test — how hard is “comfortably hard”?

RPE stands for Rate of Perceived Exertion — a simple 1–10 rating of how hard the effort feels, where 1 is strolling and 10 is all-out. No heart-rate monitor needed; you rate it by feel. The easiest way to find your number is the talk test:

  • RPE 3–4 — Easy: full sentences, breathing through your nose. Most running lives here.
  • RPE 5 — Steady: comfortable, breathing noticeably, can still chat.
  • RPE 6–7 — Comfortably hard: only short phrases between breaths. This is the effort for the 1 km repeats.
  • RPE 8+ — Hard: talking is a struggle. Save it for race day.

When the plan says “RPE 6–7,” run at the effort where you could say a few words but not hold a conversation.

Brick

Running immediately after other hard work — here, a strength circuit or erg piece. It trains the exact skill HYROX demands: running on already-tired legs. It feels heavy and awkward at first; that's the adaptation happening.

By feel

Letting how your body feels today set the effort, instead of chasing fixed numbers on a watch. It's the safest way to layer this onto a busy training week — on a flat day you go easier or walk more; on a good day it flows. Trust it.

Why not to push — the idea every book here agrees on

You don't get faster during the hard effort — you get faster afterward, when your body adapts to it. Push past the right dose and you never reach that adaptation; you tip straight into breakdown. So not pushing isn't holding back from the goal — it's the only road that reaches it. Pushing harder is the slow way. Every source in this plan says so in its own voice.

“The high rate of attrition was largely due to overtraining. Though the training mentality of that time was amazingly effective at shattering young men's perceived limitations, it was not ideal for optimal fitness.”— Mark Lauren, You Are Your Own Gym

Lauren trained Navy SEALs and Air Force PJs — and found that maximal pushing washed people out and wasn't optimal. He rebuilt the programs around sensible recovery and progression. Pushing isn't toughness; it's inefficiency.

“If the training stress is too hard, you will break down almost immediately, develop injuries, feel excessive fatigue, and fail to recover from one day to the next.”— The Runner's Body (Tucker, Dugas & Fitzgerald)

This is the mechanism. The body moves through three stages — alarm, adaptation, exhaustion — and recovery is the most important component. Stay within the right dose and you reach adaptation: stronger, faster, fitter. Overshoot and you skip straight to exhaustion — injury, fatigue, burnout.

“Less is more… it comes from relaxing muscles, opening tight joints, and using gravity to do the work instead of pushing and forcing your body to move in ways that can do it harm.”— Danny Dreyer, ChiRunning

And concretely, a few pages on: “Too much of a lean can cause your calves to clench up in pain. With the right amount… you can really begin to feel the potential effortlessness of running well.” Pushing literally manufactures the injury.

“Make it effortless, like you don't give a shit how high the hill is or how far you've got to go.”— Caballo Blanco, in Born to Run

The attitude that makes all of the above automatic: run as if the distance doesn't worry you, and it won't.

Easy. Light. Smooth. Fast.

Comfort first. The times follow.
